ORIGINAL PORTFOLIO SAMPLE

The Psychology of Procrastination

[HOOK — 0:00–0:30]

You sit down to work.

You open your laptop.

You tell yourself, "I'll start in five minutes."

Five minutes becomes thirty.

Then you check your phone.

You watch one video.

Then another.

And suddenly, two hours have disappeared.

The strange part?

You actually wanted to get the work done.

So why didn't you?

The answer may have less to do with laziness—and much more to do with how your brain responds to discomfort.

[INTRODUCTION — 0:30–1:10]

Procrastination is something almost everyone experiences. We postpone an important task, even when we know delaying it will make things worse.

We tell ourselves we'll start tomorrow. But tomorrow comes, and somehow, the task gets pushed back again.

But procrastination isn't simply about poor time management. In many situations, it's about emotion.

[SECTION 1 — WHY WE PROCRASTINATE]

Imagine you have an important project to complete.

The project isn't necessarily difficult. But it feels overwhelming.

Maybe you don't know exactly where to begin. Maybe you're afraid the final result won't be good enough. Or maybe the task is simply boring.

Your brain recognizes that discomfort and presents you with an easier alternative.

Your phone. Social media. A television show. Anything that gives you an immediate feeling of comfort.

The easier activity provides an immediate reward, while the important task offers a reward somewhere in the future.

And the human brain often prefers the reward it can have right now.

[SECTION 2 — THE FIVE-MINUTE TRICK]

Instead of telling yourself, "I need to finish this entire project today," tell yourself, "I'll work on it for five minutes."

The goal isn't necessarily to finish the task. The goal is to overcome the resistance to starting.

Once you've started, continuing often becomes easier because the psychological barrier has already been broken.

Small progress creates momentum.

[SECTION 3 — MAKE DISTRACTION HARDER]

If distractions are always within reach, you'll constantly have to rely on willpower to resist them.

So instead of asking, "How can I become more disciplined?" ask, "How can I make distraction more difficult?"

Put your phone somewhere you can't easily reach it. Close unnecessary browser tabs. Turn off notifications.

Make the behavior you want easier—and the behavior you don't want harder.

[ENDING]

The next time you catch yourself procrastinating, don't immediately call yourself lazy.

Instead, ask yourself: "What exactly am I avoiding?"

You don't need to feel ready. You just need to begin.

Because sometimes, the hardest part of getting something done... is simply beginning.

ORIGINAL PORTFOLIO SAMPLE

3 Signs You're Wasting Your Time

[HOOK — 0:00–0:05]

You might be wasting hours every day without realizing it.

And the scary part?

It doesn't always look like you're doing nothing.

[POINT 1 — 0:05–0:18]

Number one: You're constantly switching between tasks.

You start working on something, check your phone, answer a message, open another tab, then return to the original task.

Instead of trying to do five things at once, give one task your full attention.

[POINT 2 — 0:18–0:32]

Number two: You're consuming more than you're creating.

You're watching tutorials. Saving posts. Reading advice. Planning.

But you're not actually doing the thing you want to get better at.

Learning is useful—but eventually, you have to put the information into practice.

[POINT 3 — 0:32–0:46]

Number three: You're waiting for motivation.

You keep telling yourself you'll start when you feel ready. But motivation isn't guaranteed to show up.

Sometimes, you have to start first. Action creates momentum.

[CTA — 0:46–0:55]

So here's your challenge:

Put your phone away. Pick one important task. And work on it for just 20 minutes.

You might be surprised by how much you can accomplish when you stop switching and start focusing.
